# Boilerplate

Approved, ready-to-paste copy for every length. Use these as starting points. They're not sacred — they can be tightened or tuned to context — but they all pass the brand audit and they all encode the right facts.

## Taglines

### Primary tagline
> **Buildings that run themselves.**

This is the canonical one-line description of Bitpool. Use it as a homepage hero, as a closing line in pitches, as a t-shirt, as a calling card. It does the work of a positioning statement without sounding like one.

### Secondary tagline
> Tomorrow's autonomy, **shipping today.**

Used when the brand needs to lean into the "we're delivering this now, not in five years" beat. Strong for investor surfaces, conference banners, product announcements.

### Personality tagline
> Boldly **practical**. Unapologetically **real.**

The brand-personality declaration. Used in About pages, internal culture decks, the closing slide of recruitment material. Not for top-of-funnel marketing — too inward-looking for that.

### Capability taglines (one per pillar)
> **See** every signal.
> **Know** every building.
> **Act** automatically.
> **Grow** every day.

Used as section headers in product pages, slide headers in capability decks, headers in landing pages built around the SKA (See / Know / Act / Grow) promise framework.

### Warm intro

> Hi [Name],
> [Mutual contact] suggested I get in touch — they thought Bitpool would be relevant to what you're building at [their company]. Short version: we make buildings run themselves. Read me [link to one-pager]. Happy to jump on a call.
> — [Sender]

### Event follow-up

> Hi [Name],
> Good to chat at [event] yesterday. As promised, the [thing they asked about] — link to [case study / docs page]. Let me know if you'd like a deeper walkthrough.
> — [Sender]

These openers all share the same shape: name something specific they care about, name something specific Bitpool does, propose one concrete next step. No "I hope this finds you well." No "I'd love to explore how we can leverage your data."

## Email signatures

The canonical signature for outbound business email:

> [First name] [Last name]
> [Role]
> Bitpool — buildings that run themselves
> bitpool.com

No tagline competing with "buildings that run themselves." No pronoun lines unless the sender wants them (their call, brand-neutral). No quote of the week. No banner image.

## Subject lines

For outbound sales emails:

- ✅ "Bitpool + [their company]" — direct, no clickbait
- ✅ "[Specific number] sites in [region] running Bitpool" — credibility hook
- ✅ "Saw your [news item]" — reactive, specific
- ❌ "Quick question" — vague
- ❌ "Re: " when there's no prior thread — dishonest
- ❌ "🚀 Transform your building portfolio!" — every banned pattern at once

For internal team email and newsletters:

- ✅ "March product update" — boring is correct
- ✅ "Roadmap moved: docs auto-extract pushed to Q4"
- ❌ "🎉 BIG NEWS!!! 🎉" — never

## CTAs

The brand has a small inventory of standard CTAs. Use them rather than inventing new ones each time.

- **See it work** — links to a product demo or live walkthrough video
- **Read the case study** — links to a written customer story
- **Book the call** — links to the sales calendar
- **See the docs** — links to documentation
- **See the status** — links to the status page
- **Hire us** — links to careers
- **Talk to us** — links to a contact form or sales address

Don't use: "Discover more," "Learn how," "Click here," "Find out," "Get started" (use a more specific verb).

## Feature announcement template

When announcing a new capability — in changelog, in a release note, in a blog post — use this template:

> **[Verb]. [What it does in three words].**
> [One short sentence explaining what's new and why it matters to the customer.]
> [Optional: one short sentence with the specific outcome or behaviour change.]
> [Live now / Rolling out this week / Available to all customers on the [tier] plan.]

Worked example:

> **Auto-tune. Set and forget.**
> Setpoints now adjust themselves overnight based on the previous day's occupancy. No more weekly schedule meetings; the building handles it.
> Live now, every site, no setting to enable.

## Customer success metric templates

When writing about a customer outcome, use this shape:

> [Specific outcome with number]. [Time frame]. [Named site or anonymised real instance]. [Optional: how Bitpool delivered it].

Worked examples:

> 30% less energy. Year one. Westpac Place. Setpoint optimisation across all 38 floors.

> £142,400 saved. Twelve months. A retail chain in the South East. Catching chiller faults before tenants complained.

> 14 hours back. Per week. The facilities team at a 40-storey commercial tower in Sydney. No more manual schedule maintenance.

## What not to put in the boilerplate

The temptation is to expand boilerplate to cover every audience and use case. Resist. The boilerplate is sharp because it's small. If you find yourself wanting a 13th tagline or a 4th 50-word description, ask whether you actually need a new variant or whether the existing one works for the new context.

Things that don't belong in approved boilerplate:

- ❌ Tier-specific marketing claims that might change next quarter ("the only platform that...")
- ❌ Comparative claims about competitors ("better than X")
- ❌ Forward-looking statements that aren't already on the public record
- ❌ Customer names that haven't been cleared for use in marketing
- ❌ Specific revenue, valuation, or employee-count figures (these change too fast; the only canonical numbers in approved boilerplate are 60 billion records, 500+ buildings, $2.5M ARR target by 2027 — confirm with the team before using these in any new context as the numbers themselves will move)

If you're not sure whether a number or claim is safe to use externally, ask. The cost of asking is always lower than the cost of retracting.
