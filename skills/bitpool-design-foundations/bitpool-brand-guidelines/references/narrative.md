# Narrative

The story Bitpool tells about itself. This file isn't a script to recite — it's the source material every other piece of communication draws from. Read it before writing anything long-form, before pitching to a new audience, or before stress-testing whether a piece of copy is authentic to the brand.

## The one-sentence story

Buildings have always been dumb. Bitpool makes them smart. Quietly.

That's it. Every longer narrative is a variation on that beat — the problem (buildings are dumb), the change (Bitpool fixes that), the manner (quietly, without dashboards).

## The longer story

Commercial buildings run on industrial control systems designed in the 1980s. Every chiller, every air handler, every meter speaks a slightly different language. Most of the time those signals go nowhere — into a dashboard nobody opens, into an alarm queue nobody triages, into a log file nobody reads. The data is there. The intelligence isn't.

Building owners have lived with this for forty years. They've hired facilities engineers to manage the gap by hand. They've bought "smart building" point solutions that promised dashboards and delivered more dashboards. They've been told AI was coming and watched it arrive as another dashboard. Every layer added is another login, another tab, another thing for the facilities team to babysit.

Bitpool is the platform that doesn't need babysitting. We collect every signal — from every controller, every vendor, every vintage — and we use it. The platform learns what each building does, surfaces what matters, and acts on what it learns. Setpoints get tuned overnight. Faults surface before tenants notice. Energy spend drops, year on year. Nobody logs in.

This is what autonomy actually looks like. Not a demo. Not a future quarter. Real buildings, today, running themselves.

## The positioning statement

For **building owners and operators** who are **tired of dashboards that don't act**, Bitpool is the **autonomous building intelligence platform** that **runs your buildings for you** — unlike traditional BMS, point analytics tools, or consultancy-led optimisations, Bitpool ships software that acts on its own.

Decode:

- **Audience**: owners and operators, not vendors. We sell to the people who pay the energy bill, not to the people who install the controllers.
- **Pain we name**: dashboards that don't act. This is the consensus complaint across the industry.
- **Category we claim**: autonomous building intelligence. New language. Not "BMS," not "analytics," not "fault detection."
- **What's different**: we ship software that acts. We don't recommend; we do.

This positioning is the anchor. If a piece of copy drifts away from it — into "AI for buildings," into "smart building dashboards," into "energy management consultancy" — bring it back.

## What category are we in (and what we're not)

Bitpool is sometimes filed by analysts and prospects in one of four adjacent categories. None of them is quite right:

- **Building Management Systems (BMS)** — Bitpool sits on top of the BMS, reads its data, and acts through it. We don't replace it. Vendors here are Siemens, Honeywell, Schneider, Johnson Controls. Don't pretend to compete with them as a control system. Position Bitpool as the intelligence layer.
- **Energy management software** — Bitpool does this but it's a single feature, not the product. If a prospect frames Bitpool as energy management, broaden the frame to autonomy.
- **Fault detection and diagnostics (FDD)** — Bitpool does this too, again as a feature. The category was hot in 2015–2020 and has aged poorly; many FDD vendors are now repositioning as "AI." Don't anchor to FDD unless the prospect is specifically shopping for it.
- **Smart building platforms / digital twins** — overlapping but distinct. Most "smart building" platforms still demand a human user. Bitpool's distinguishing claim is autonomy: the platform you don't log into.

When asked "are you a BMS, an FDD, an energy tool, or a smart building platform?" the answer is: **we're the autonomous layer that sits on top of all of them.** The category we're trying to establish is autonomous building intelligence.

## Who the customer is

Three audience tiers, in priority order:

### 1. The owner-operator of a real estate portfolio
A corporate occupier or property owner with 10+ buildings under one balance sheet. Pays the energy bill directly. Has a facilities team they want to free up. Cares about a single number per building (energy spend, comfort complaints, tenant satisfaction). This is the buyer.

### 2. The head of facilities at a major site
The person actually responsible for keeping the building running. Currently drowning in dashboards and alarms from five different vendors. Wants the building to stop being a 2am phone call. This is the day-one user — and the strongest internal champion if won over.

### 3. The energy / sustainability lead
Often newer in the org. Has board pressure to report carbon and energy figures. Currently relies on consultants and spreadsheets. Bitpool gives them real numbers without needing to chase. This is the secondary buyer; usually rides along on a deal led by #1 or #2.

We don't sell to:

- ❌ IT departments (they don't pay the energy bill and don't suffer the dashboard fatigue)
- ❌ Controls vendors (they're integration partners, not customers)
- ❌ Pure consultancies (sometimes resellers, never primary buyers)
- ❌ Residential or single-building owners (the platform's economics need scale)

## How we talk about competitors

The brand's default posture is to not. Bitpool wins by being clearly different, not by being better at the incumbent's game. In practice:

- **Never name competitors in marketing copy.** Don't write "unlike BuildingIQ" or "where Switch Automation falls short." It reads as defensive and feeds the competitor's SEO.
- **In sales conversations**, when a prospect names a competitor, characterise the difference structurally rather than disparagingly. "They're a dashboard-led product; we're autonomous. They get installed by the facilities team; we get installed once and then run." Then move on.
- **In analyst briefings** be candid about the category landscape — the analyst's job depends on a credible competitive map and bluffing damages your credibility. But still don't disparage; name the structural difference.
- **In RFP responses** answer the question asked and don't volunteer comparisons. If the RFP forces a side-by-side feature matrix, fill it in honestly and let the structural differences speak.

The brand's confidence comes from doing the thing, not from running down others. Stay above it.

## How we talk about the market

The building intelligence market is at an inflection. Three forces are converging:

1. **Energy and carbon regulation** — every major jurisdiction is mandating real-time energy reporting; the data has to come from somewhere
2. **The maturity of cloud telemetry** — building controllers can finally stream data reliably enough to be useful; this wasn't true ten years ago
3. **The economic squeeze on facilities teams** — owners are cutting headcount and demanding more from the building stock at the same time

Bitpool's claim to be the autonomous layer is structurally well-timed: the data is finally available, the labour is finally scarce enough to justify automation, and the regulators are finally demanding the kind of evidence Bitpool produces. State these forces when needed but don't belabour them — they're the wind at the brand's back, not the brand itself.

We are not "riding the AI wave." Bitpool was building this before the current AI cycle began and will be building it after the cycle resets. The brand should sound that way: durable, focused on the building, not on the tech trend.

## The founding story

Bitpool was started by [founder name(s) and brief origin]. The team came out of [industry context] and built the first version of the platform because [the specific pain point that drove it]. The first customer was [name]. The platform has been in production for [duration] and currently runs in 500+ buildings across three continents.

(This skill encodes the brand voice but doesn't fix specific personal-history facts — these change with hiring, milestones, and corporate history. Confirm the canonical founding-story sentences with the team before using them in any high-stakes context.)

What's load-bearing about the founding story for the brand:

- It came from real buildings, not a whiteboard.
- It was built by people who had felt the pain themselves.
- It's been in production long enough to have learned the hard lessons.

When telling the story externally, lean on these three beats. Don't lean on funding rounds, growth percentages, or the technology stack.

## Brand archetype

If brand archetypes help: Bitpool sits between **The Sage** (knows the building better than anyone) and **The Creator** (builds the thing that makes the building work). Not The Hero (Bitpool doesn't save anyone, the customer is the hero). Not The Magician (Bitpool isn't mysterious — the platform is honest about what it does). Not The Everyman (Bitpool is opinionated and specialist).

In practice this means the writing should sound like a senior engineer who deeply understands the domain and has built the thing they're describing. Not like a marketer. Not like a wizard. Like the person who could answer any technical question put to them and chooses not to make a big deal of that.

## How the brand handles uncertainty and bad news

The brand is honest about what's hard. When the platform misses something, the brand says so. When a customer churns, the brand learns from it. When a feature ships late, the brand doesn't hide the slip.

Specific patterns:

- **Status page incidents**: written in plain English with a timeline and a root cause. No corporate hedging. "We had a 14-minute outage in our message broker. Devices kept buffering locally so no data was lost, but live updates lagged. Fixed. Here's what we changed."
- **Roadmap delays**: announced before customers notice. "Auto-extract was scheduled for Q3 and has slipped to Q4. The model wasn't accurate enough at the threshold we'd set. We'd rather ship later than ship wrong."
- **Acknowledging a competitor advantage**: rare but sometimes necessary. "Yes, they're stronger on retail today; we're stronger in commercial offices. If retail is your category, talk to them."
- **Apologising**: short, specific, no fluff. "We got this wrong. Here's what we're doing about it."

The brand earns trust by being honest in moments where most companies hedge. This is the same instinct as the "specific over vague" pillar — it just plays out in difficult conversations rather than marketing copy.

## How the brand handles success

The mirror image. When Bitpool wins, the brand states the win specifically and doesn't crow.

- ✅ "Closed our largest enterprise deal this quarter — 80 sites across Southeast Asia."
- ❌ "🚀🚀🚀 BIG WIN! Just signed our biggest deal EVER!! Game-changing moment!! 🎉"

State, don't perform. Brag with numbers, not adjectives.

## Putting it all together

When you're writing a piece of communication for Bitpool, ask:

1. **Who am I writing to?** (Owner-operator, facilities lead, sustainability lead, investor, analyst, prospect, customer, recruit, journalist?)
2. **What is the one thing I want them to take away?** (One thing, not three.)
3. **What's the specific evidence?** (Number, named site, real outcome.)
4. **What voice mode does this context want?** (Strategic, product, sales, customer, technical, internal.)
5. **Have I stayed within the positioning?** (Autonomous, not dashboards. Acts, not recommends. The platform you don't log into.)

If you can answer all five sharply, the writing will be on-brand. If any of the answers are vague, the writing will be vague — and the brand audit will catch it.

The brand is not a list of rules. The brand is the discipline of doing the above five things every time you write.
